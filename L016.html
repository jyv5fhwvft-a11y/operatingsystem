🎯 The Core Problem — The Storage Gap

Memory is nanoseconds. Storage is milliseconds.

What DRAM gives us

Main memory access: ~100 ns. From a 3 GHz CPU's perspective, that's ~300 cycles of "hurry up and wait." Volatile — lost on power off. Expensive per gigabyte.

What storage gives us

HDD: ~10 ms seek + read. That's 100,000× slower than a DRAM access. Non-volatile — survives power off. Cheap per gigabyte.

SSD: ~50–100 µs read. Still 500–1000× slower than DRAM — but 100× faster than HDD.

The bridge — the OS

The operating system exists partly to hide this gap: page cache, write-back buffering, read-ahead prefetch, and the block device layer all exist to make storage feel faster than it is.

💡
The design consequence: every layer between application and disk — filesystems, page cache, block scheduler — is built around minimizing the number of physical seeks. That's why sequential I/O is 100× faster than random I/O on HDDs.

🎵 Analogy 1 · The Record Player Needle

Getting to the song takes real time

The setup

Imagine a classic vinyl record player. To play a specific song, you must physically lift the tonearm, move it to the correct track position (Seek Time), and wait for the vinyl record to spin around until the opening note passes right underneath the needle (Rotational Latency).

What's happening physically

Seek = move the tonearm to the right groove
Rotate = wait for the right part of the groove to pass under the needle
Transfer = actually read the electrical signals as the groove scrolls past

The critical cost

Moving metal takes real, physical time. The tonearm has momentum. It must decelerate to stop, change direction, and accelerate again. That's why even a "fast" drive has a baseline 4–15 ms penalty per seek.

💡 Analogy 1 · The Grid of Light Switches

No moving parts. But wear out with use.

The setup

A Solid-State Drive (SSD) is like a massive grid of millions of light switches on a wall. To read a sector, you don't move anything — you just read the electrical state of a switch instantly.

What's happening physically

Each NAND flash cell holds a charge (or doesn't) representing bits. Reading means measuring that charge with a sense amplifier — no mechanical motion, no waiting for rotation. Fast, silent, shock-resistant.

The catch — wear

Flipping a switch too many times wears out the plastic. Similarly, NAND cells degrade after a limited number of program/erase cycles (P/E cycles) — typically 3,000–10,000 for MLC, 100,000 for enterprise SLC. Once worn out, the cell can't reliably hold a charge.

The mitigation

Since some switches wear faster than others, the building manager rotates which switches are used over time. This is Wear Leveling — distribute writes evenly so no single cell dies early.

The analogy

Disk geometry optimization matches low-level audio buffer rendering arrays, sorting whether incoming block seek vectors are processed in raw arrival order or grouped using elevators to minimize sweeping the physical arm head.

The elevator (SCAN) algorithm

Like an elevator that keeps going up to the top floor before coming down, the disk head sweeps in one direction, servicing all requests in its path, then reverses. This dramatically reduces total head movement.

The win

Same number of requests. Same amount of data. But 30–70% less head movement because requests are processed in physical proximity order, not arrival order.

💡
Where this lives: the Linux mq-deadline and BFQ block schedulers use variants of SCAN. SSDs don't need it — random access has the same cost as sequential.
🧭 System Cohesion — Where L16 Sits

The physical reality under every filesystem

Relies On · Module 4 (Sync)

Every disk operation must coordinate with concurrent processes — reads, writes, and seeks all contend for the same physical head. Locking patterns in filesystem code are the most deadlock-prone in the kernel.

Feeds Into · L17 (File Abstractions)

The extreme performance difference between sequential reads and random sweeps on mechanical drives directly explains why classic Unix file system layouts obsess over contiguous disk block groupings.

The insight that drives everything else

Because random access is 100× slower than sequential on HDD, every storage layer above the disk — filesystem allocation, page cache eviction, block scheduler, read-ahead prefetch — is optimized to transform random I/O into sequential I/O.

💡
Historical context: the design of classic UNIX (inode block pointers, contiguous data runs, extents) dates from the 1970s, when "disk" meant a spinning platter. Modern SSDs break many of these assumptions — but the abstractions remain.

📖 Core Breakdown — HDD Anatomy

Platters. Tracks. Sectors. Moving metal.

The components

Platter — magnetic disk spinning at 5400–15000 RPM
Spindle — center axle turning all platters together
Track — concentric ring on a platter surface
Sector — fixed-size arc segment (typically 512 B or 4 KB)
Actuator arm — moves the read/write head across tracks

The physical movement

Every read requires two physical motions: swinging the arm to the correct track (radial) and waiting for the correct sector to rotate under the head (angular). Neither is instantaneous — both consume milliseconds.

The heads are tiny

Read/write heads fly just nanometers above the platter surface. This is why HDDs are fragile — a shock can crash the head into the platter and destroy data. Also why HDDs have a parking mechanism.

📖 Core Breakdown — Access Time = Three Phases

Seek + Rotate + Transfer. Every read.

The three-phase cost
1 · SEEK TIME4–15 ms
move arm
2 · ROTATIONAL LATENCY2–5.5 ms
wait for sector
3 · TRANSFER TIME0.005 ms
data
total = seek + rotate + transfer
≈ 8–20 ms per random access
Seek dominates

The head has to physically move from its current track to the target track. Average seek = 1/3 of full stroke (assuming random target). On a 7200 RPM drive, that's typically 4–9 ms.

Rotate is next

Once on the right track, wait for the platter to rotate so the target sector is under the head. Average rotation delay = half a rotation — that's 4.17 ms at 7200 RPM.

Transfer is tiny

Once the head is in position, the actual data transfer is nearly free — tens of microseconds for a few KB. The physics of getting to the data dwarfs the cost of reading it.

💡
The 100× rule of thumb: 99.9% of the access time is spent moving the arm and waiting for rotation. Less than 0.1% is spent transferring data. That's why reducing seeks matters so much.
📖 Core Breakdown — Seek Time

The radial motion of the arm

What it is

The physical time required for the HDD's mechanical read/write head arm to sweep and lock onto a target track cylinder. It's the radial movement of the arm from its current position to the destination track.

Why it's slow

Acceleration — the arm has mass; it must be accelerated by a voice coil
Travel — moving across tracks takes time proportional to distance
Deceleration — it must stop precisely on the target track
Settling — the head vibrates slightly and must stabilize before reading

The numbers

Desktop drives: 8–15 ms average seek. Enterprise 15K RPM drives: 2–4 ms. Typical HDD spec: "average seek time" assumes random target = 1/3 of full stroke. Full-stroke seek times are 3× higher.

♿
Micro-Accessibility Context: SSDs have no seek time — there's no arm, no radial distance. Any random address costs the same as any other. This is the single biggest architectural difference between HDD and SSD.

📖 Core Breakdown — Rotational Latency

The angular wait for the sector

Geometry of rotational latency
What it is

The duration a drive partition waits for a specific sector block to spin under the active magnetic head sensor. Once the arm is on the right track, the head must wait for the target sector to rotate into position.

The math

RPM ÷ 60 = rotations per second
1 ÷ rotations/sec = seconds per rotation
× 1000 = milliseconds per rotation
÷ 2 = average rotational latency

The numbers by RPM

5400 RPM → 11.11 ms/rot → avg 5.56 ms
7200 RPM → 8.33 ms/rot → avg 4.17 ms
10000 RPM → 6.00 ms/rot → avg 3.00 ms
15000 RPM → 4.00 ms/rot → avg 2.00 ms

📖 Core Breakdown — Transfer Time

The only fast part

What it is

The actual data flow rate from the platter, through the head, through the drive controller, over the bus, into memory. Expressed as megabytes per second (MB/s) or gigabytes per second (GB/s).

The math

transfer_time = bytes / transfer_rate

Example: 512 bytes at 100 MB/s
= 512 / 100,000,000
= 0.00000512 s = 0.00512 ms

Typical rates

HDD (modern): 100–250 MB/s
SATA SSD: 500–550 MB/s
NVMe SSD (PCIe 4): 3–7 GB/s
NVMe SSD (PCIe 5): 10–14 GB/s

💡
Why "small random reads" are catastrophic: if you read 512 bytes at a time, transfer = 0.005 ms. But seek + rotate = 8 ms. You spend 99.9% of your time positioning the head and 0.1% doing useful work. That's why databases and filesystems love sequential access.

📖 Core Breakdown — SSD Anatomy

NAND flash. Pages and blocks.

NAND flash layout
A FLASH BLOCK = 128–512 PAGES
ERASING = WHOLE BLOCK ONLY
To rewrite one page, the whole block
(128–512 pages) must be erased first
→ expensive: 2–5 ms per block erase
The hierarchy

Cell — holds 1–4 bits (SLC/MLC/TLC/QLC)
Page — 4–16 KB, the smallest read/write unit
Block — 128–512 pages, the smallest erase unit
Plane/Die — multiple blocks operating in parallel

The asymmetry

You can read a page independently, and write a page that hasn't been written since the last erase. But you cannot overwrite a page — you must first erase the entire block it lives in.

Why this matters

Every small overwrite forces the FTL (Flash Translation Layer) to do a read-modify-write of a whole block. That's why random small writes to SSDs are slower than sequential, and why SSDs have a "write amplification" factor.

📖 Core Breakdown — The Read/Write Asymmetry

Reads are fast. Writes are slow. Erases are brutal.

Reads — cheap

To read a page, the controller measures the charge level of each cell. ~50–100 µs. No modification, no wear. Reading is wear-free.

Writes (programs) — moderate

To write a fresh page, the controller sets the charge level. ~200–500 µs. Writes consume one P/E cycle of the target cells.

Erases — expensive

To erase a whole block, the controller must clear every cell at once. 2–5 ms per block. Every erase consumes one P/E cycle across the whole block.

💡
The result: SSDs are optimized for large sequential writes because that amortizes the block-erase cost. Small random writes trigger "write amplification" — the same physical byte gets rewritten many times as blocks are repeatedly erased and rewritten.
Read vs. Write vs. Erase latencies

PAGE READ

50–100 µs

Just measure cell charge. Wear-free. Can happen at full random speed.

PAGE WRITE

200–500 µs

Set charge level. Consumes 1 P/E cycle. Only works on an already-erased page.

BLOCK ERASE

2–5 ms

Whole 128–512-page block at once. Consumes 1 P/E cycle per cell. The bottleneck.

📖 Core Breakdown — FTL & Wear Leveling

The hidden translator that saves the drive

The Flash Translation Layer (FTL)

The embedded controller software inside an SSD that translates virtual OS sector read commands into physical cell array electrical voltage updates. It's essentially the SSD's own tiny OS.

What it does

Logical → Physical mapping: LBA 1024 might live at physical page 7 of block 3
Garbage collection: consolidate valid pages, erase fully-dead blocks
Wear leveling: spread P/E cycles evenly across all blocks
Bad block management: mark cells that have failed and remap around them

Wear leveling strategies

Dynamic: only pick free blocks with low wear when writing
Static: periodically move cold data (rarely-accessed) to higher-wear blocks to balance
The goal: every block hits its P/E limit at roughly the same time, so the whole drive dies at once — not prematurely

♿
Micro-Accessibility Context: modern SSDs typically last 5–10 years of normal use thanks to wear leveling. Without it, a heavily-rewritten block could fail in weeks while the rest of the drive is 99% healthy.

🧪 Live Demo — Wear Leveling in Action

Watch how the FTL spreads writes evenly

What you're watching

Each cell is a NAND page with a wear counter. On every write, the FTL picks a cell with the lowest current wear to spread load. Watch the wear climb — but stay roughly balanced across all cells.

The comparison

Without wear leveling, the same 10 cells would get hammered while the other 54 stayed fresh. With wear leveling, all 64 cells accumulate wear at approximately the same rate — giving the drive many more total write cycles.

Real-world numbers

An MLC SSD rated for 3000 P/E cycles can write 3000 × 500 GB = 1.5 PB before the first cells wear out. With wear leveling, the drive stays usable until most cells wear out — much longer than 1.5 PB in practice for most workloads.

💡
The engineering insight: the SSD's raw NAND has a much smaller capacity than its advertised capacity. The spare area (over-provisioning) gives the FTL room to do wear leveling and garbage collection without ever running out of free blocks.

📖 Real World — Why Sequential Beats Random

Access patterns dominate performance

Sequential vs random on HDD
Sequential I/O

Read 100 KB from one contiguous region. One seek, one rotation wait, then the head streams 100 KB directly. ~1 ms total. Effective throughput ~100 MB/s.

Random I/O

Read 20 × 4 KB from scattered locations. Twenty seeks, twenty rotational waits. ~160 ms total. Effective throughput ~0.5 MB/s — 200× slower.

Why filesystems care

Classic Unix filesystems (ext2, ext3, ext4) allocate file data in contiguous extents. Reading a file sequentially is fast; reading it after fragmentation is slow. This is why defrag existed and why filesystem design obsesses over locality.

💡
SSDs change this — partially: random 4 KB reads on SSD cost ~100 µs, so 20 of them = 2 ms vs sequential 1 ms. Still 2× — but not 200×. That's why SSD-era filesystems (btrfs, zfs, f2fs) don't obsess over contiguous allocation the way older ones did.

The formula, in code

disk_access.c
#include <stdio.h>// Compute HDD access time for a random readdouble compute_access_time(    double rpm,                // e.g. 7200    double avg_seek_ms,        // e.g. 4.0    double transfer_mbps,      // e.g. 100 (MB/s)    double bytes                // e.g. 512) {    // Rotational latency = half of one rotation    double rot_per_sec = rpm / 60.0;    double ms_per_rot  = 1000.0 / rot_per_sec;    double avg_rot_ms  = ms_per_rot / 2.0;    // Transfer time = bytes / rate, converted to ms    double rate_bps   = transfer_mbps * 1e6;    double xfer_ms     = (bytes / rate_bps) * 1000.0;    return avg_seek_ms + avg_rot_ms + xfer_ms;}int main() {    double t = compute_access_time(7200, 4.0, 100, 512);    printf("Access time: %.3f ms\n", t);    return 0;}

What to notice

The bytes parameter barely matters. Changing it from 512 to 128 KB only adds ~1.3 ms of transfer — the seek + rotate cost stays the same. That's the fundamental lesson: positioning dominates.

🛑 Three Student Pitfalls

What beginners get wrong about disk physics

PITFALL 01
"Transfer rate is what matters for disk speed"
Transfer rate is the peak rate — achieved only for large sequential reads. For random 4 KB reads, transfer is less than 0.1% of the access time. Seek + rotate dominate. A 200 MB/s HDD doing random 4 KB reads achieves roughly 500 KB/s — the transfer rate is meaningless for that workload.
PITFALL 02
"SSDs don't have seek time, so they're equally fast for all I/O"
Not quite. SSDs have no mechanical seek, so random 4 KB reads cost the same as sequential 4 KB reads (both ~100 µs). But write workloads are different: random small writes cost much more than sequential writes, because each small write can trigger a block erase + read-modify-write. Random write amplification can make SSDs 5–10× slower for random vs sequential writes.
PITFALL 03
"More disks means more storage means faster"
Adding disks (RAID, distributed storage) improves throughput (parallel reads across drives) but not latency. A single 4 KB read still costs ~8 ms on any one HDD. If your workload is latency-bound (databases, message queues), adding disks doesn't help — you need SSDs or caching.
🎭 Interactive Activity — The Defragmentation Challenge

Count the seeks. Then defragment.

The setup

Below is a fragmented disk layout. The colored blocks are files; the empty blocks are free space. A large file (the red blocks) is scattered across the disk, with free blocks interspersed.

The task

1. Count how many times the head must seek to read the file in order (left to right).
2. Propose a defragmentation: move the free blocks to consolidate the file.
3. Count again — how many seeks after defrag?

The debrief

Before: 6 seeks (one per fragment). After: 1 seek (one contiguous read). Defragmentation reduces seek count by 6× — and reduces access time proportionally.

💡
SSDs change this calculus: since SSDs have no seek time, fragmented files on SSDs cost almost nothing extra. This is why modern SSDs don't need defragmentation — and why running defrag on an SSD only wastes its P/E cycles.

The insight that shapes everything else

Because random I/O is dramatically more expensive than sequential on HDDs, filesystems are designed around contiguity — inode block pointers assume the file's blocks are adjacent; extents (ext4) allocate runs of blocks; the block allocator tries to keep a file's blocks clustered.

What SSDs changed

SSDs remove the seek penalty, but introduce erase-block management. Modern filesystems (f2fs, btrfs, zfs) are designed for flash: they cluster writes, avoid overwrites when possible, and use TRIM to help the FTL reclaim blocks.

🏆 Grand Summary — L16 Key Takeaways

Four things to walk out with

01

HDD access time = seek + rotate + transfer. For small reads, seek and rotate dominate — 99.9% of the time is moving metal, not transferring data.

02

Rotational latency = half a rotation on average. At 7200 RPM that's 4.17 ms; at 15000 RPM it's 2 ms. Seek time is 4–15 ms depending on drive class.

03

SSDs have no seek, no rotate — random 4 KB reads cost ~100 µs instead of ~8 ms. But NAND wear (P/E cycles) and erase-block asymmetry introduce their own constraints.

04

Wear leveling and the FTL hide NAND's limitations: they map logical addresses to fresh physical pages, spread P/E cycles evenly, and enable garbage collection. Without them, SSDs would die quickly.
