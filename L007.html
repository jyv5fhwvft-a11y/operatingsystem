🎯 What Does the CPU Scheduler Actually Do?

It picks one READY process and gives it the CPU core

The decision

The scheduler decides which process in the READY state gets to slide onto an active processor core next. From L05: only processes in READY are candidates.

When it fires

1. A process blocks on I/O (RUNNING → WAITING)
2. A process exits (RUNNING → TERMINATED)
3. A timer tick preempts the current process
4. A new process arrives and has higher priority

The three questions every policy answers

Which one? — the priority rule.
How long? — the time quantum (if any).
When to switch? — cooperative or preemptive.

💡
Scheduling is a policy layer. The context switch mechanism (L06) is the mechanism. Scheduling is the policy that decides when to invoke it.

🛒 Analogy 1 · The Grocery Store Checkout Line

One cashier. Three policies. Three very different lines.

First-Come First-Served (FCFS)

Check people out based on when they joined the line. If a person with a massive cart stands in front, the person buying a single pack of gum is trapped waiting forever — the convoy effect.

Shortest-Job-First (SJF)

Sort the line so the person with the single pack of gum goes first. Minimizes average wait time — but requires magically predicting how long each customer will take.

Round Robin (RR)

The cashier spends exactly 30 seconds processing a customer's items. If they aren't done, they're forced to walk to the very back of the line and wait for their next turn — the time quantum fairness.

💡
The tradeoff: FCFS is fair in arrival order but slow. SJF is fast but requires prediction. RR is responsive but burns time on switches.

🌐 Analogy 2 · Network Packet Routing Ring Buffers

Scheduling policies are traffic-shaping algorithms in disguise

FCFS ≈ FIFO queue

A simple ring buffer — packets leave in the exact order they arrived. No reordering, no prioritization. Suffers from a "long packet" (jumbo frame) blocking everything behind it.

SJF ≈ priority queue by size

Sorts packets by size, sends smallest first. Minimizes average queueing delay. Requires knowing each packet's size in advance — always true for network frames.

RR ≈ deficit round robin

Each flow gets a fixed byte quantum per round. If a packet doesn't finish, it goes back to the queue. Guarantees fairness — this is literally how Deficit Round Robin (DRR) works in real routers.

📖 Core Breakdown — The Three Foundational Algorithms

One question, three answers

FCFS

Queue by arrival time. Run each process to completion before starting the next. Non-preemptive. Simple. Trivial to implement.

⚠ Convoy effect

SJF

Pick the process with the shortest burst time. Non-preemptive (in this form). Provably optimal for average wait. Requires burst time prediction.

⚠ Starvation for long jobs

Round Robin (RR)

Give each process a fixed quantum. Preempt and rotate when it expires. Responsive for interactive workloads. Requires timer interrupts.

⚠ Quantum tuning matters

The dimension they differ on

Preemption. FCFS and SJF are cooperative — once a process starts, it runs to completion. RR is preemptive — the timer forces a switch.

📊 The Three Metrics — What We're Optimizing

Three numbers that grade every policy

Metric 01
Turnaround Time
Total time from arrival to completion. "How long did the customer wait in the store?"
= completion − arrival
Metric 02
Waiting Time
Time spent in READY (not running). "How long was the customer stuck in line?"
= turnaround − burst
Metric 03
Response Time
Time from arrival to first execution. "How long until the cashier said hello?"
= first_run − arrival
Which matters most?

Batch systems care about throughput and average turnaround. Interactive systems care about response time — you want the shell to echo your keystroke instantly, even if the total time to completion is long.

What the exam will test

Given a workload and a policy, compute all three for each process and the averages. In L07's practical example, we'll do exactly this for the three-algorithm workload.

🔵 FCFS — First-Come First-Served

The simplest possible scheduler. Also the worst for mixed workloads.

The rule

Processes are placed in a FIFO queue by arrival time. When the CPU is free, take the head of the queue and run it to completion. Non-preemptive — once it starts, it finishes.

The convoy effect

A long process at the front of the queue blocks every short process behind it. On a mixed workload — say one 24 ms job followed by ten 1 ms jobs — the ten short jobs wait the full 24 ms.

Why it still matters

FCFS is the conceptual baseline every other policy is measured against. It's also the default when nothing else works — some schedulers degrade to FCFS when overloaded.

🚗 The Convoy Effect — Why FCFS Fails

A single long job paralyzes every short job behind it

The scenario

One 24 ms job followed by nine 1 ms jobs, all arriving at t=0. FCFS runs the 24 ms job first. The nine 1 ms jobs wait 24 + 25 + 26 + ... ms. Total system time: 33 ms with most wasted on the convoy.

The average wait

FCFS: (0 + 24 + 25 + 26 + 27 + 28 + 29 + 30 + 31 + 32) / 10 = 25.2 ms. SJF: (0 + 1 + 2 + ... + 8 + 24) / 10 = 7.9 ms. A 3× improvement from reordering alone.

Where it hurts most

Interactive workloads. If your terminal, browser, and music player are all blocked behind a big compile job, the whole desktop stutters for 30+ seconds.

💡
The convoy effect is why preemption exists. If you can't reorder, at least you can force the long job to yield periodically — that's what RR does.

The rule

Pick the process with the shortest burst time. Non-preemptive in this form — once it starts, it runs to completion. Requires knowing burst time in advance.

Why it's optimal

Reordering moves short jobs forward, and the arithmetic mean of wait times is minimized by the shortest-first order. Provably optimal — no policy can beat SJF on average wait for a non-preemptive queue.

The catch

The scheduler can't actually know the burst time in advance. Real SJF systems use exponential averaging of past burst times as a heuristic prediction.

⚠️ SJF — The Starvation Problem

Short jobs always win. Long jobs may never run.

The failure mode

If short jobs keep arriving, the long job at the back of the queue never gets picked. On a busy server, a background data export could be starved indefinitely by a constant stream of 1-ms interactive requests.

The mitigation — aging

Gradually increase a process's priority the longer it waits. After enough time, even a 24 ms job can out-prioritize the fresh 1-ms jobs. Aging converts SJF's starvation into a temporary delay.

The tradeoff

Aging partially defeats SJF's optimality — you're no longer always choosing the shortest job. But it prevents the pathological case where a job never runs. In practice, aging is the standard fix.

💡
Same starvation problem hits every priority scheme. Any time you can indefinitely prefer some class of jobs, you risk the other class never running. L08 dives into aging in detail.

🟢 SJF Variant — Shortest Remaining Time First (SRTF)

Preemptive SJF — new short jobs can interrupt running ones

The rule

When a new process arrives, compare its burst time to the current running process's remaining time. If the new one is shorter, preempt — even mid-execution.

When it wins

SRTF can beat SJF's average wait when jobs arrive at different times. Example: if a 5 ms job is running and a 1 ms job arrives, SRTF switches to the 1 ms job immediately, shaving 4 ms off its wait.

The cost

Every preemption is a context switch. If short jobs arrive constantly, the system may spend more time switching than doing work. Starvation is also worse — the long job gets interrupted continuously.

♿
Micro-Accessibility Context: SRTF is mostly of theoretical interest — no production OS uses it. But it's a common exam question because it tests whether you understand preemption vs. non-preemption.

🟡 Round Robin — Preemptive Fairness

The time quantum is the entire policy

Live · The quantum rotation

Each process gets one quantum per round. Preemption is timer-driven.

The rule

Each process gets a fixed time quantum (typically 1–100 ms). When the quantum expires, the process is preempted and moved to the back of the ready queue. Next process gets its turn.

Why it's fair

No process waits longer than (n−1) × quantum before running again, where n = number of ready processes. Bounded wait time — that's the killer feature for interactive systems.

Why it's preemptive

The timer interrupt fires at the end of every quantum. That interrupt preempts the current process even if it's mid-computation. No cooperation required — RR works on any process, including buggy ones.

💡
RR + L06: every quantum expiry is a context switch. That's why quantum tuning matters so much — too small, and you spend all your time saving/loading registers.

⚖️ The Quantum Tradeoff

Quantum is a dial between fairness and overhead

⚠ Quantum too large

If the quantum is bigger than most processes' burst times, they finish in one slice — and Round Robin degenerates into FCFS. You lose responsiveness and re-inherit the convoy effect.

Example: q = 100 ms on a workload with 5 ms jobs → effectively FCFS

⚠ Quantum too small

If the quantum is tiny compared to the switch cost, the system spends most of its time context switching rather than running processes. Throughput collapses.

Example: q = 100 µs on a 2 µs switch → 2% overhead per switch, but 2000 switches/sec
The rule of thumb

Quantum should be slightly larger than the typical interactive task. If most tasks finish in 5–10 ms, use q = 20–50 ms. Enough to complete most jobs in one slice, small enough to keep preemption responsive.

The typical numbers

Linux CFS (Completely Fair Scheduler) uses targeted latency ≈ 6–24 ms with dynamic quantum per task. Windows uses ~15 ms. macOS uses ~10 ms. All within an order of magnitude.

💻 Code — Implementing a Basic Scheduler

The core of any scheduler in 20 lines

scheduler.c
// Simplified scheduler · picks the next process to runtypedef struct {    int pid, burst, remaining, arrival;    enum {READY, RUNNING, DONE} state;} task_t;// FCFS: pick the earliest-arrived READY tasktask_t* pick_fcfs(task_t *tasks, int n) {    task_t *best = NULL;    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {        if (tasks[i].state != READY) continue;        if (!best || tasks[i].arrival < best->arrival)            best = &tasks[i];    }    return best;}// SJF: pick the shortest READY task (compare burst)task_t* pick_sjf(task_t *tasks, int n) {    task_t *best = NULL;    for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {        if (tasks[i].state != READY) continue;        if (!best || tasks[i].burst < best->burst)            best = &tasks[i];    }    return best;}
What's identical

Both policies have the same loop — scan every READY task, keep a pointer to the "best" one so far. The only difference is the comparison key: arrival time for FCFS, burst time for SJF.

What's missing

This is the policy layer only. A real scheduler also needs: a way to invoke it on a timer (RR's preemption), a ready queue data structure, and the context switch mechanism itself.

The generalization

If you replace the comparison with "highest priority" you get a priority scheduler. Replace it with "smallest remaining time" you get SRTF. Replace it with "least recently run" you get a fairness-biased policy. Every scheduler is a comparison function.

🌍 Real World — Linux's Completely Fair Scheduler

The modern production scheduler — a very fancy RR variant

The core idea

Track each task's virtual runtime (vruntime) — roughly how much CPU time it has consumed. Always run the task with the smallest vruntime. This gives absolute fairness in the long run.

How it differs from textbook RR

No fixed quantum. Instead, CFS picks a targeted latency (typically 6–24 ms) and divides it proportionally among runnable tasks. Two tasks → each gets ~50% of the latency. Ten tasks → each gets ~10%. Dynamic quantum per task.

The efficient data structure

All runnable tasks live in a red-black tree keyed on vruntime. Picking the next task is O(log n) — finding the leftmost node. Adding a task is O(log n). This scales to thousands of threads.

💡
What this means for you: CFS's near-perfect fairness is why your music doesn't skip even when the CPU is saturated. It's also why a runaway process makes every other process slower — not just the ones behind it in a queue.

🛑 Three Student Pitfalls

What beginners get wrong about scheduling

PITFALL 01
"SJF is always best because it minimizes average wait"
SJF minimizes average wait assuming you know burst times and every job eventually runs. It causes starvation and is unimplementable without burst prediction. Production systems almost never use pure SJF — they use RR variants with priority aging.
PITFALL 02
"Round Robin has no starvation"
True for tasks in the same priority class, but a high-priority process can still starve a low-priority one if there's a strict hierarchy. And RR's guarantee is that every ready task gets a turn — but a task blocked on I/O isn't ready, and can be bypassed for a long time.
PITFALL 03
"The response time is the same as the wait time"
They're different. Response time = arrival to first dispatch. Wait time = total time in READY across all dispatches. A process could respond instantly but then be preempted repeatedly, giving huge wait time with tiny response time.
🏷️ System Lexicon — Preemption & Quantum
Preemption
Term 01 of 04
Formal Definition

The ability of an operating system to forcefully pause a running process to hand the CPU core to a different task.

Plain Translation

( The kernel's ability to hit pause on a running program — without asking. )

Which policies preempt?

RR and SRTF do. FCFS and basic SJF don't — they run each process to completion.

Time Quantum
Term 02 of 04
Formal Definition

The maximum continuous block of time a thread is permitted to run under a preemptive scheduling policy before being context-switched.

Plain Translation

( The timer that says "you've had enough CPU for now — step aside." )

Typical values

1–100 ms. Linux CFS uses ~6–24 ms targeted latency. Windows ~15 ms.

🏷️ System Lexicon — Convoy & Metrics
Convoy Effect
Term 03 of 04
Formal Definition

A system slowdown scenario where short processes wait a prolonged duration for a long, heavy process to vacate the CPU core.

Plain Translation

( One slow truck blocks a highway of fast cars. )

Which policy causes it?

FCFS — the strictest offender. It's the entire reason preemption exists.

Avg Wait Time
Term 04 of 04
Formal Definition

The arithmetic mean of the total time processes spent sitting inside the READY queue waiting for execution access.

Plain Translation

( On average, how long did your program sit in line? )

Formula

wait[i] = completion[i] − arrival[i] − burst[i]
avg = sum(wait) / n

🏆 Grand Summary — L07 Key Takeaways

Four things to walk out with

01

The scheduler picks which READY process gets the CPU. Three foundational policies: FCFS (arrival order), SJF (shortest burst), RR (time quantum).

02

FCFS is trivial but suffers the convoy effect — one long job blocks everything behind it. Average wait: 17 ms in the canonical example.

03

SJF is provably optimal for average wait (3 ms in the same example), but requires burst-time prediction and causes starvation.

04

Round Robin provides bounded wait time via preemption. The quantum size is the entire policy — too big degenerates to FCFS, too small burns on context switches. Modern Linux CFS is a highly evolved RR variant.
